## Science

Pluto is a dwarf planet of the Kuiper Belt, the region of icy bodies beyond Neptune. It is smaller than our Moon (2,377 km in diameter) but far from being an inert rock: the New Horizons probe revealed plains of nitrogen ice that flow like glaciers, mountains of water ice more than 3 km high, a thin, layered, bluish atmosphere and perhaps an ocean hidden beneath the crust.

The large bright heart-shaped region is called Tombaugh Regio; its western half, Sputnik Planitia, is a basin filled with nitrogen ice churned by slow convection currents, with a surface free of craters and therefore very young. Pluto and its moon Charon almost form a double planet: the center of mass of the system falls outside Pluto, and the two always show each other the same face.

## History

On 18 February 1930 Clyde Tombaugh, a 24-year-old hired at the Lowell Observatory in Arizona, found Pluto by comparing photographic plates of the same area of sky taken a few days apart. The name was suggested by Venetia Burney, an 11-year-old girl from Oxford who loved mythology.

In 2006 the International Astronomical Union, meeting in Prague, defined for the first time what a planet is, and Pluto became a "dwarf planet": it has not "cleared" its orbit of other bodies. On 14 July 2015 New Horizons flew past it at 12,500 km, delivering the first detailed images.

## Fun facts

- A year on Pluto lasts 248 Earth years: since its discovery it has not yet completed one orbit around the Sun; it will do so in 2178.
- A small part of Clyde Tombaugh's ashes travels aboard New Horizons.
- Pluto has five moons: Charon, Nix, Hydra, Kerberos and Styx.
- Its astronomical symbol, an intertwined "P" and "L", also recalls the initials of Percival Lowell, who had long searched for a ninth planet.
- Its distance from the Sun varies a lot: between about 30 and 49 times that of the Earth.
