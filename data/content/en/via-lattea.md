## Science

The Milky Way is our galaxy: a disk of stars, gas and dust with a central bulge crossed by a bar, all embedded in an almost spherical halo of old stars, globular clusters and dark matter. It contains between 100 and 400 billion stars. The mass of the stars alone is estimated at about 50-60 billion solar masses, while the total mass, dominated by the dark matter we cannot see, is several hundred billion solar masses, perhaps a trillion: the estimates depend on the method.

The stellar disk is about 100,000 light years across but very thin: the layer containing most of the stars is about a thousand light years thick, so in proportion it is as thin as a CD. Young stars and gas gather in the **spiral arms**: four main arms (Perseus, Scutum-Centaurus, Sagittarius-Carina, Norma-Outer) and smaller segments such as the Orion Arm, where the Sun lies.

The Sun is about 27,000 light years from the center, where the black hole Sagittarius A* hides. It travels along its orbit around the center of the Galaxy at about 230-240 km/s and takes a little more than 200 million years for a full turn: the last time the Sun was at this point of its orbit, the first dinosaurs were appearing on Earth.

The model you see in Atlante Celeste is a reconstruction: nobody has ever photographed the Milky Way from outside. The positions of the arms in the observed stretches come from the parallaxes of masers in star-forming regions (Reid and collaborators, 2019); the opposite side of the Galaxy, hidden behind the center, is a plausible extension.

## History

As early as the 5th century BC, Democritus suggested that the Milky Way was made of countless stars too faint to be seen one by one. The confirmation came in 1610: Galileo pointed his telescope at the glowing band and in the *Sidereus Nuncius* wrote that it was "a mass of innumerable stars".

In the 18th century Thomas Wright and Immanuel Kant realized that its band-like shape comes from the fact that we live inside a disk. William Herschel counted the stars in hundreds of directions to map its shape (1785), but interstellar dust hid the distant regions from him and the Sun ended up almost at the center. In 1918 Harlow Shapley, studying globular clusters, moved the center of the Galaxy towards Sagittarius, tens of thousands of light years away.

In 1920 the "Great Debate" between Shapley and Heber Curtis discussed whether the spiral nebulae were other galaxies: Edwin Hubble settled the question in 1924-25 by measuring the distance to Andromeda. In the 1950s radio astronomy, picking up the 21 cm line of hydrogen, revealed the spiral structure. Today ESA's Gaia satellite has measured the position and distance of almost two billion stars.

## Fun facts

- The name comes from the Greek myth of the milk spilled by Hera while she was nursing Heracles; "galaxy" also comes from the Greek *gála*, milk.
- From Italy the brightest part, towards the galactic center in Sagittarius and Scorpius, can be seen in summer low in the south, far from city lights.
- The Milky Way has swallowed many smaller galaxies: Gaia found the stars of an ancient merger, called Gaia-Enceladus, which happened about 10 billion years ago. The Sagittarius dwarf galaxy is still being torn apart today.
- The dark patches along the band, such as the "Cygnus Rift", are not empty: they are dust clouds hiding the stars behind them.
- Andromeda is approaching at about 110 km/s, and in a few billion years the two galaxies might merge: the most recent simulations, based on Gaia and Hubble data, give about a one-in-two chance that it happens within 10 billion years.
