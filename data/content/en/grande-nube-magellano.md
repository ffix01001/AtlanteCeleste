## Science

The Large Magellanic Cloud is a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way, about 160,000 light years away (49.6 kiloparsecs, measured in 2019 with 1% precision). It is an irregular galaxy with a bar, rich in gas and young stars, with a mass of about a tenth of the Milky Way's.

It hosts the Tarantula Nebula (30 Doradus), the most active star-forming region in the whole Local Group of galaxies: at its center lies the cluster R136, with some of the most massive stars ever observed, up to about 200 times the Sun.

Measurements by Hubble and Gaia indicate that the Magellanic Clouds move so fast that they are probably on their first passage near the Milky Way.

## History

Visible to the naked eye from the southern hemisphere as a little cloud detached from the Milky Way, it has been known to southern peoples since time immemorial. It was described by the Persian astronomer al-Sufi in 964 in his *Book of Fixed Stars*. In Europe it became known through Magellan's voyage: **Antonio Pigafetta** from Vicenza, who took part in the first circumnavigation of the globe (1519-1522) and wrote its journal, described the two "little clouds" of the southern sky.

On 23 February 1987 the supernova SN 1987A exploded there, the closest observed since Kepler's time (1604): it was the first supernova whose neutrinos were detected, picked up a few hours before the light arrived.

## Fun facts

- It is not visible from Italy: it lies at about 70° south declination.
- Its distance, measured with eclipsing binary stars, is one of the fundamental "rungs" on which the measurement of the expansion of the Universe is based.
- In Atlante Celeste the Large Cloud is drawn as a glowing cloud: a star-based model like the one of the Milky Way is one of the possible next steps.
