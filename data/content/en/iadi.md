## Science

The Hyades are the open cluster closest to the Solar System, about 153 light years away (47 parsecs), in the constellation Taurus. They form the "head" of the Bull, the characteristic V shape. They are about 650 million years old and contain a few hundred stars moving together through space.

The bright orange star Aldebaran seems to belong to it, but this is a trick of perspective: it lies halfway, about 65 light years away, and has nothing to do with the cluster.

## History

The name comes from Greek mythology: the Hyades were the nymph sisters of Hyas, linked to rain, because their rising coincided with the rainy season. Precisely because they are close and their stars move together, throughout the 20th century the Hyades were the first rung of the cosmic "distance ladder": their distance was derived from their motion, and from there the luminosity of stars was calibrated.

## Fun facts

- During the total solar eclipse of 29 May 1919, the Sun was right in front of the Hyades: Arthur Eddington's expeditions measured the apparent shift of their stars near the edge of the Sun and confirmed the bending of light predicted by Einstein's general relativity.
- The Gaia satellite discovered that the Hyades are slowly losing stars: two long "tidal tails" stretch for hundreds of light years ahead of and behind the cluster.
