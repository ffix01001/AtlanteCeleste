## Science

The Moon is the fifth largest natural satellite in the Solar System and, relative to its planet, the largest among those of the rocky planets. It is thought to have formed about 4.5 billion years ago from a colossal impact between the early Earth and a Mars-sized body called Theia: the debris thrown into orbit would have come back together to form the Moon.

The dark patches, the "seas" (maria), are ancient expanses of basaltic lava; the bright areas are older highlands, densely cratered. The Moon rotates synchronously and always shows us the same face, but thanks to librations, small apparent wobbles, about 59% of it can be seen from Earth. Water ice has been found in the permanently shadowed polar craters, a precious resource for future bases.

## History

In 1609–1610 Galileo observed mountains and craters through his telescope, discovering that the Moon was not the perfect sphere imagined by Aristotle. The names we still use today (Sea of Tranquility, Sea of Serenity, Copernicus crater...) come from the Bologna Jesuits Giovanni Battista Riccioli and Francesco Maria Grimaldi, who published them in 1651.

The Soviet Luna 2 (1959) was the first human-made object to reach it, and Luna 3 photographed its far side. On 20 July 1969 Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin, with Apollo 11, were the first to walk on it; in all, twelve astronauts visited it between 1969 and 1972. In recent years China has landed on the far side (Chang'e 4, 2019) and brought back samples from it (Chang'e 6, 2024), India has reached the regions near the south pole (Chandrayaan-3, 2023), and NASA is preparing the return of human beings with the Artemis program.

## Fun facts

- The "far side" is not dark: it receives the same sunlight as the other side, we just never see it from Earth.
- The Moon is moving away by about 3.8 cm per year: this is measured with lasers aimed at the mirrors left by the Apollo astronauts.
- The Sun and the Moon have almost the same apparent size in the sky, which is why total eclipses exist. It is a temporary coincidence: in a few hundred million years the Moon will be too far away to cover the Sun completely.
- The lunar surface reflects only about 12% of the light: it is roughly as dark as worn asphalt.
- The Apollo seismometers recorded shallow "moonquakes" that can last more than ten minutes, because the Moon's dry rock keeps vibrating for a long time.
- With no wind or rain, the astronauts' footprints could remain intact for millions of years.
