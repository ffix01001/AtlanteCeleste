## Science

Neptune is the planet farthest from the Sun, at about 30 times the Earth–Sun distance. Like Uranus it is an ice giant, but it is much more active: its winds are the fastest in the Solar System and exceed 2,000 km/h. It emits about 2.6 times the energy it receives from the Sun, the sign of an internal heat source that is still partly mysterious.

In 1989 Voyager 2 photographed the Great Dark Spot, an Earth-sized storm that the Hubble Space Telescope found had already disappeared by 1994. Neptune has thin rings; in the outermost, the Adams ring, there are clumps of material called "arcs", named Liberty, Equality, Fraternity and Courage. Its largest moon, Triton, orbits backwards: it is probably a captured Kuiper Belt object.

## History

Neptune is the first planet discovered thanks to mathematics. The orbit of Uranus showed small irregularities; the Frenchman Urbain Le Verrier calculated where a planet capable of causing them should be and sent the prediction to the Berlin observatory. The very evening he received the letter, on 23 September 1846, Johann Galle found Neptune less than a degree from the indicated point. Similar calculations had been made in England by John Couch Adams.

Galileo had already observed it between December 1612 and January 1613, drawing it as a star near Jupiter, without noticing that it moved. The only close visit is still that of Voyager 2, on 25 August 1989.

## Fun facts

- In July 2011 Neptune completed its first full orbit since its discovery: a Neptunian year lasts almost 165 Earth years.
- Between 1979 and 1999 Pluto was closer to the Sun than Neptune. An orbital resonance prevents the two bodies from ever getting too close.
- Deep inside Neptune the carbon of methane, squeezed by enormous pressures, could form "diamond rain" (a hypothesis supported by laboratory experiments).
- The deep blue of the Voyager 2 photos had been enhanced to bring out details: reconstructions from 2024 show a paler blue, similar to that of Uranus.
