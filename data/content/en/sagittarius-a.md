## Science

At the center of the Milky Way, about 27,000 light years from us, there is a supermassive black hole: Sagittarius A* (pronounced "Sagittarius A star"). It has a mass of about 4.3 million times that of the Sun, packed into a region smaller than the orbit of Mercury: its event horizon, the boundary from which not even light can escape, has a radius of about 12.7 million kilometers.

We know this by watching the stars that orbit around it. The most famous, S2, completes one orbit in 16 years on a very elongated path: at its closest approach, in May 2018, it came within about 120 times the Earth-Sun distance, traveling at more than 7,500 km/s, 2.5% of the speed of light. On that occasion the GRAVITY instrument measured the gravitational redshift of its light, and later the slow rotation of its orbit (Schwarzschild precession), exactly as general relativity predicts.

Compared with the black holes of other galaxies, Sgr A* is very quiet: it swallows very little gas and shines faintly, with small flares almost every day in the infrared and in X-rays. But it has not always been like this: the "Fermi bubbles", huge lobes of hot gas extending for tens of thousands of light years above and below the galactic disk, are probably the sign of violent activity a few million years ago.

## History

The radio source was discovered in 1974 by Bruce Balick and Robert Brown with the Green Bank interferometer; Brown gave it the name with the asterisk in 1982, to distinguish it from the rest of the Sagittarius A region.

From the 1990s two groups, Reinhard Genzel's (Max Planck Institute, with ESO's telescopes in Chile) and Andrea Ghez's (UCLA, with the Keck telescopes in Hawaii), followed for decades the orbits of the stars near the center, showing that an enormous, invisible mass sits there. In 2020 they received the Nobel Prize in Physics, shared with Roger Penrose.

On 12 May 2022 the Event Horizon Telescope, a network of radio telescopes as large as the Earth, revealed the first image of the shadow of Sgr A*: a ring of light around a dark center. In Atlante Celeste, from far away, you see it just like that, blurred as the radio telescopes see it and with the colors of the EHT images (which works with 1.3-millimeter radio waves: the colors only show the intensity).

As you get closer the image becomes sharp, as general relativity describes it: a black shadow 2.6 times the size of the event horizon, a very thin ring of photons that circle the black hole before escaping, and the hot gas brighter on the side where it rotates towards us. All around, gravity bends the light of the stars behind it: the sky is distorted into arcs, and where the images merge the "Einstein ring" forms. The stars are those of the nuclear star cluster, millions of stars packed into a few light years around the center of the Galaxy.

In Atlante Celeste, up close, all of this is computed by really following the light rays through the curved spacetime of a rotating black hole. How fast Sgr A* actually spins has not yet been measured with certainty (the EHT images and the flares suggest that it spins, perhaps quickly): here it starts at 90% of the maximum possible and you can change it with the slider in the panel. With rotation the shadow shifts and flattens on one side, and near the horizon the ergosphere appears, a region where space itself is dragged around and nothing can stay still. The hot gas is a thick, transparent flow, brighter on the side coming towards you. During the flares you will see a "hot spot" circling the black hole in about 50 minutes: this is what the GRAVITY instrument observed in 2018, at about 4 times the horizon radius and at 30% of the speed of light. The "Near Sagittarius A*" panel tells you what you would feel hovering at that point: how much your clock slows down, how much thrust you would need not to fall, how big the shadow is in the sky.

## Fun facts

- Seen from Earth, the shadow of Sgr A* has the same apparent size as a donut lying on the Moon.
- Between us and the galactic center there is so much dust that, in visible light, only about one part in a thousand billion of the emitted light reaches us: that is why it is observed in the infrared, in radio waves and in X-rays.
- Although it is "super" massive, Sgr A* is rather a lightweight: the black hole at the center of the galaxy M87 has about 6.5 billion solar masses.
- The gas around Sgr A* rotates so fast that it goes around the black hole in a few minutes (around M87's it takes days or weeks): that is why photographing it was harder than for M87.
- Gravitational lensing is best seen up close: at about twenty radii from the horizon, light passing next to the black hole is deflected by tens of degrees.
- Move a few hundred times the Earth-Sun distance away: you will see the S stars racing around the black hole along their real orbits, measured over thirty years of observations. Speed up time to watch them go around.
