## Science

The Sun is a G2V star, a middle-aged "yellow dwarf": it formed about 4.6 billion years ago and contains 99.86% of all the mass of the Solar System. In its core, at almost 16 million degrees, it fuses about 600 million tons of hydrogen into helium every second; the difference in mass, about 4 million tons per second, becomes energy according to the relation E = mc².

From the inside out we find the core, the radiative zone, the convective zone (where the gas boils like in a pot), the photosphere that we see as the "surface" (about 5,500 °C), the chromosphere and finally the corona. The corona exceeds a million degrees: why it is so much hotter than the surface is one of the great open problems of solar physics.

The Sun's activity follows a cycle of about 11 years, marked by the number of sunspots. The wind of particles that the Sun emits non-stop inflates a bubble, the heliosphere, which envelops all the planets and extends well beyond Pluto.

## History

Between 1610 and 1612 Galileo Galilei observed sunspots through his telescope and, following their motion, showed that the Sun rotates. In the 19th century spectroscopy made it possible to read its chemical composition: helium was discovered in the solar spectrum in 1868, almost thirty years before it was found on Earth, and it is named after the Sun itself (in Greek *helios*).

In September 1859 the astronomer Richard Carrington saw a flare with his own eyes: the geomagnetic storm that followed, the most intense ever recorded, lit up auroras as far as the Caribbean and knocked out the telegraphs. Today the Sun is watched by a fleet of probes, including SOHO, Solar Orbiter and Parker Solar Probe, which in 2021 entered the corona for the first time.

## Fun facts

- The light you see now left the Sun about 8 minutes and 20 seconds ago, but the energy that powers it was born in the core much earlier: photons bounce around in the radiative zone for an estimated tens to hundreds of thousands of years.
- The Sun is white, not yellow: the warm color we see comes from the Earth's atmosphere, which scatters the blue part of the light.
- It does not rotate like a rigid body: at the equator it completes a turn in about 25 days, near the poles in about 35.
- In 4.6 billion years it has "burned" only about 0.03% of its mass.
- The barycenter of the Solar System, the point around which everything revolves, often falls outside the Sun, mainly because of the pull of Jupiter and Saturn. In the Atlas the Sun really moves around that point.
- In about 5 billion years it will swell into a red giant and then slowly fade as a white dwarf.
