## Science

Halley's comet is the most famous of the periodic comets: it returns close to the Sun about every 75–76 years, on a very elongated, retrograde orbit that takes it from inside the orbit of Venus to beyond that of Neptune. Its nucleus, measured by the European probe Giotto, is a block of ice and dust about 15 × 8 km, very dark: it reflects only 4% of the light, like coal.

Every pass close to the Sun makes it lose material. The dust fragments left along its orbit produce two meteor showers: the Eta Aquariids in May and the Orionids in October.

## History

In 1705 Edmond Halley noticed that the comets observed in 1531, 1607 and 1682 had almost identical orbits and predicted that the same object would return in 1758. Halley died in 1742; the comet reappeared right on time on Christmas night 1758 and has borne his name ever since. It had already been recorded by Chinese and Babylonian astronomers, and it appears in the Bayeux Tapestry (1066).

Around 1305 Giotto di Bondone painted a comet in place of the star of the Magi in the Scrovegni Chapel in Padua, probably inspired by Halley's passage of 1301. That is why the ESA probe that in March 1986 passed less than 600 km from the nucleus was called Giotto.

## Fun facts

- Its last perihelion passage was on 9 February 1986; the next will be on 28 July 2061. In December 2023 the comet reached the farthest point of its orbit and began its journey back.
- Mark Twain was born in 1835, during a passage of the comet, and predicted that he would go out with the next one: he died in 1910, the day after perihelion.
- In 1910 the Earth passed through the comet's tail, and some unscrupulous sellers sold "anti-comet pills".
